What this is: A solo entry to NTU's veNTUre programme, hosted by C22 — a behavioral-finance company that profits by detecting predictable mistakes in retail traders and taking the opposite side of their trade ("fading") in real time. C22 gave 34 campaigns (Feb–Jul 2026) of anonymized 24-hour gold-trading-challenge data. The task: find exploitable behavioral patterns, build a validated strategy framework, recommend rule-design changes, and — for Stage 4 — a real-time working prototype. Judged 30% technical rigor / 30% business value / 30% practical implementation / 10% innovation, against ~50 other participants, 3 cash prizes.
Data handling: The traders' identity file contained raw emails and IP addresses despite being described as anonymized. This was flagged to C22 immediately, and every pipeline run since sanitizes it on load — emails are one-way hashed into opaque IDs, IPs are converted into anonymous cluster numbers (never stored or read as real addresses), and nothing raw is committed or shared.
Core economic finding: C22's "fade" P&L (reverseProfit) turns out to be an exact formula: trader's gross profit, reversed, minus a flat $7 cost per lot traded. That single fact reframes the whole problem — instead of "are traders good or bad," the real question becomes "in which situations does a trader's expected loss exceed $7 per lot," because that's the only place a fade is profitable. It also explained the traders' own average −$42.75 result: their actual trading skill is close to breakeven (median trade is slightly profitable before costs); it's C22's own commission structure that produces almost all of that loss.
Main behavioral finding (statistically confirmed): After two consecutive losing trades, a trader's next trade loses about $40/lot on average — nearly six times the $7 break-even line. This held up under two independent robustness tests: a trader-level bootstrap and a much stricter "worst-case" bootstrap that clusters by shared internet connection instead of by person, in case some accounts aren't truly independent. Both gave essentially the same answer, so this is the strongest single piece of evidence in the dataset.
Other behavioral edges found (positive but not yet individually proven, will feed a combined model): trading without a stop-loss, re-entering a trade in the same direction after being stopped out, quickly re-entering after a loss, increasing position size after losses, and trading late in a losing session — all shift outcomes the same direction as the confirmed trigger, just with smaller individual samples.
Structural discoveries useful to C22 beyond the trading strategy itself:
* The median trader's account resolves (passes or blows up) in about 2 hours, not the full 24-hour window — meaning C22 collects less behavioral data per campaign than the challenge length suggests.
* Something changed between campaign 52 and 53: participation jumped roughly fivefold, stop-loss usage dropped 25 percentage points, and trader skill flipped from profitable to unprofitable — a clear before/after split that needs an explanation from C22, and is itself evidence that changing the rules changes participant behavior (which is one of C22's own stated interests).
* Only about 6-8% of registered participants ever actually place a trade.
* A meaningful share of active traders return across multiple campaigns and cluster on shared IP addresses — investigated in depth and found to be ordinary shared infrastructure (mobile/office networks), not coordinated multi-accounting, based on a synchrony test showing no unusual timing correlation across the vast majority of these clusters.
